Why letter-by-letter doesn't work

Thai leaves many vowels unwritten, writes some vowels before the consonant, and has letters that are not pronounced. Each word has to be split into syllables, with every consonant read as either an initial or a final, before it can be romanized:

คนkhonNo vowel written; an o goes between the consonants

เพื่อนphueanOne vowel written in three pieces: before, above and after

How to use it

  1. Paste Thai into the box. The romanization appears below as you type, with spaces between words.
  2. To practise sentence by sentence, paste a whole passage (a lesson, a dialogue or song lyrics) and turn on Line by line: each Thai line is followed by its romanization.
  3. Choose how syllables are written above: hyphenated (sa-wat-di) to read one syllable at a time, or joined (sawatdi) as on road signs and maps.

FAQ

Why are there no tone marks?

RTGS does not mark tones. Thai has five tones, but the official system writes only consonants and vowels; it is what you see on road signs, place names and passports. To practise tones, follow the tone marks in the Thai text.

Why do long and short vowels look the same?

RTGS does not distinguish vowel length: ข้าว (rice) and ขาว (white) are both khao. Listen to the original audio for vowel length.

Why does it differ from how Thai people spell their names in English?

Many personal and shop names use their own spellings, such as v for ว, j for จ, or letters based on the original Sanskrit. This tool follows RTGS, which spells by pronunciation, so it will differ from those.

Does it ever split words wrongly?

Common words are mostly split correctly. Word splitting uses the Thai dictionary built into your browser, and syllables are worked out from Thai spelling rules. Names, new words and loanwords are occasionally split wrongly, and in Pali and Sanskrit loanwords where one consonant is pronounced twice (ผลไม้ phon-la-mai), only the common words it knows come out right.

Can I use it for song lyrics?

Yes. Paste the whole song and turn on Line by line: each Thai line is followed by its romanization. Hyphenated syllables make it easier to match the words to the melody.
